# Urban Curb Digital Twin

A multi-agent, discrete-event digital twin of a downtown district in which
**passenger cars, delivery vehicles and ridehail (TNC) vehicles compete for the
same scarce curb space** — plus the calibration and optimization machinery needed
to say something defensible about what curb policy should do about it.

The model answers questions of the form: *if 20% of metered stalls become
loading zones, who gains, who loses, and by how much?* — with confidence
intervals, a held-out validation, and a Pareto frontier instead of a single
number.

```
      passenger cars        delivery vehicles          ridehail
   long dwell, will walk   short dwell, won't walk   very short dwell,
      price-sensitive      time-expensive, will      no walking at all,
            │              double-park                     │
            └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                                   ▼
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │   ~980 curb stalls, 80 block │
                    │   faces, three regulation    │
                    │   types, priced by location  │
                    └──────────────┬───────────────┘
                                   ▼
        cruising ── double-parking ── lane blockage ── congestion
                                   ▼
                      network-wide social cost
```

> **This is a synthetic model.** The network, the demand and the "observed"
> occupancy used for calibration are all generated by this repository. Nothing
> here is empirical data about Los Angeles or any other city, and no number
> below should be read as a measurement of a real place. See
> [Data and limitations](#data-and-limitations).

## Why curb competition

The curb is the only part of the street network where every mode has to stop.
A commuter parks there for an hour, a courier stops for eleven minutes, a TNC
stops for ninety seconds — and they are all trying to do it on the same fifty
metres of frontage, at the same time of day, next to the same building.

Three things make this hard to reason about without a model:

1. **The modes are coupled through one capacity constraint.** More TNC pickups
   do not just make TNC pickups worse; they take stalls that parked cars would
   have used, which lengthens cruising, which puts more cars on the link.
2. **Failure is externalised.** A driver who cannot find a stall does not
   disappear. They cruise (congestion, emissions), or they double-park (a lane
   blocked for everyone behind them). The cost of a curb shortage mostly lands on
   people who were not looking for curb space.
3. **There is no single objective.** Loading-zone conversion is good for freight
   and bad for parking. Any honest answer is a frontier, not an optimum.

This model makes all three explicit and measurable.

python -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate

```

                       │           behaviour, scenarios, objective
        ┌──────────────┴───────────────┐
        ▼                              ▼
  src/simulation/                 src/sumo/         (optional backend)
  ┌────────────────────┐          ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ routing.py  graph  │          │ network.py  SUMO XML gen │
  │ curb.py     stalls │          │ backend.py  TraCI loop   │
  │ environment.py     │          │ dispatch.py taxi device  │
  │ engine.py   SimPy  │          └──────────────────────────┘
  └─────────┬──────────┘                      │
            │        both backends score curb options with the
            │        SAME function: agents.base_agent.curb_choice_score
            ▼
  src/agents/  passenger.py · delivery.py · ridehail.py
            │
            ▼
  src/experiments/   metrics.py · runner.py (parallel) · provenance.py
            │                              · sensitivity.py
            ├──────────────► src/calibration/  fit behaviour to occupancy
            └──────────────► src/optimization/ objective · baselines · search
                                       │
                                       ▼
                             results/ + src/viz/ (HTML report, dashboard)
```

Design decisions worth naming:

**One configuration, two backends.** `config/network.yaml` is turned into a
NetworkX graph by the SimPy engine and into SUMO node/edge/parkingArea XML by
`src/sumo/network.py`. If the two backends had separate inventories, any
difference between their results would be uninterpretable. A test asserts the
stall counts match exactly.

**`run_simulation(cfg)` is pure.** Same config in, same raw output out; no
filesystem, no globals, no hidden state. Everything above it — multi-seed
experiments, calibration objectives, optimization — is just repeated calls to
that one function, which is what makes parallelism and reproducibility fall out
for free rather than being retrofitted.

**Recording is separate from aggregation.** The engine stores facts (this trip
searched for 4.1 minutes and ended illegally); `experiments/metrics.py` turns
facts into statistics. A stored raw run can be re-summarised with new metrics
without re-simulating.

**Configuration is hashable.** `RunConfig.fingerprint()` is a stable hash of
everything except the seed, so replications of the same experiment group
themselves, and a result can always be traced back to the exact inputs that
produced it.

## The behavioural model

All three classes choose curb space by minimising the same generalised cost —

```
cost = α · walking_distance
     + β · price × expected_dwell
     + γ · expected_search_time
     + δ · expected_occupancy_of_the_block
```

— and differ entirely in the weights and in what they do when the search fails.
That is the whole behavioural content of the model, and it lives in one function
(`src/agents/base_agent.py::curb_choice_score`) used by both backends.

| | passenger car | delivery vehicle | ridehail |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean dwell | 55 min | 14 min service | 74 s pickup, 38 s drop-off |
| acceptable walk | 320 m | 90 m | 80 m (the *passenger* walks) |
| α (walk, $/m) | 0.0075 | 0.045 — six times more distance-averse | 0.012 |
| γ (search, $/min) | 0.30 | 0.95 — driver time is expensive | 1.30 — circling is pure waste |
| value of time | $18/h | $42/h | $26/h |
| eligible stalls | metered | loading zone → metered | TNC zone → metered |
| when search fails | comply (accept a long walk anywhere in the district) or park illegally, per a calibrated compliance probability | double-park, per a calibrated propensity — the operationally rational choice | circle the block up to 3 times, then double-park |

The asymmetry between a 55-minute passenger dwell and a 74-second TNC stop is
the engine of curb competition here: one parked commuter removes a stall from the
pool for roughly forty TNC pickups' worth of curb time.

**Ridehail is modelled as a fleet, not as independent trips.** Requests arrive,
a dispatcher assigns the nearest idle vehicle, the vehicle deadheads to the
pickup, competes for curb space, boards, carries and drops off. Fleet size is
derived from demand once at construction time, so a demand-shock scenario cannot
silently improve service quality by conjuring extra vehicles. Passenger wait time
is measured from request to boarding, so it absorbs dispatch queueing, deadhead
*and* curb search — which is what a rider actually experiences.

**Double-parking is a physical event, not a counter.** It registers a blockage on
the link, which raises the travel time of every other vehicle using it. This is
the channel through which a curb shortage becomes a network-wide congestion cost,
and it is why the objective has a VMT term at all.

## Simulation engine

- **Discrete-event, in SimPy.** Time is minutes; distances metres; money dollars.
  Every metric's unit is in its name.
- **Curb capacity is tracked explicitly, not with `simpy.Resource`.** A `Resource`
  is a *queueing* primitive: a request that cannot be served waits. Curb space
  does not work that way — a driver who finds a block full balks and drives to
  the next one, and that cruising is the phenomenon the model exists to measure.
  The time-of-day scenario also changes a segment's regulation mix mid-run, which
  a fixed-capacity `Resource` cannot express.
- **Non-homogeneous Poisson arrivals by thinning**, so the time-of-day profile is
  exactly Poisson rather than approximately so.
- **Warm-up is taken seriously.** The district starts empty, which is a state no
  real downtown is ever in. The first 90 minutes (long relative to the 55-minute
  mean dwell) are discarded: statistics are reset and trips that *arrived* during
  warm-up are excluded. Sampling also stops at the horizon, because demand stops
  there and the model then drains — including the drain-down would bias every
  occupancy statistic toward zero. Getting this wrong is the single easiest way
  to produce a model that looks fine and is optimistic about everything.
- **Congestion** is a BPR volume-delay function over a rolling 15-minute flow
  window, plus a fixed per-vehicle delay for each double-parked vehicle on the link.
- **District-level stall apportionment.** With ~12 stalls per block face, rounding
  each face independently means a face can only express allocation shares in steps
  of about 8% — so district-wide allocations differing by less than that collapse
  onto identical physical inventories, making the optimizer's objective surface
  flat in places and jumpy in others for reasons that have nothing to do with curb
  policy. Stalls are therefore apportioned at the district level and repaired
  segment by segment, hitting the requested shares to within one stall. This was a
  real bug found while probing the objective surface, and it is the kind of thing
  a single-notebook implementation hides.

## Experiments and reproducibility

```bash
python -m src.experiments.runner --scenario baseline --seeds 30
python -m src.experiments.runner --all --seeds 30 --workers 8
python -m src.experiments.runner --benchmark --seeds 16
python -m src.experiments.runner --list
```

Every replication is described entirely by its `RunConfig` and its seed, so:

- **A run is reproducible from its seed alone.** A test asserts that the same
  seed produces bit-identical output, that a different seed does not, and that a
  run does not depend on what was simulated before it.
- **Parallel and sequential agree exactly.** Workers re-materialise the config
  from a serialised dict rather than inheriting live objects, so behaviour is
  identical under `fork` and `spawn` (Linux, macOS, Windows). A test asserts the
  numbers match.
- **Every experiment writes a manifest** — git commit, whether the tree was
  dirty, the resolved config and its hash, the seed list, Python version,
  platform, CPU count, wall time. `results/<scenario>/manifest.json`.
- **The README's numbers are generated from the results, not typed.**
  `scripts/render_results.py` rewrites the [Results](#results) section from the
  CSV/JSON the pipeline wrote; `--check` fails if they have drifted apart.

Replications are independent, so the workload is embarrassingly parallel and is
distributed across processes with `concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor`.
Speedup is measured, not assumed — see [Parallel scaling](#results).

## Calibration and validation

A city can observe *how full each curb segment is* far more easily than it can
observe *why drivers behave the way they do*. Calibration therefore treats the
segment-level occupancy profile as the observable and searches a small,
interpretable behavioural parameter vector for the values that best reproduce it.

```bash
python -m src.calibration.calibrate --generate   # build synthetic observations
python -m src.calibration.calibrate --seeds 3 --workers 8
```

Seven free parameters, deliberately few — the identifiability of a simulation
model degrades quickly as the parameter vector grows relative to the information
in the observations:

| parameter | interpretation |
|---|---|
| `passenger_alpha_walk` | how far a driver will park from the door |
| `passenger_gamma_search` | search time traded against walking |
| `passenger_delta_occupancy` | avoidance of blocks that look full |
| `passenger_search_radius` | size of the considered choice set |
| `passenger_compliance` | probability of continuing to search rather than parking illegally |
| `delivery_illegal_prob` | courier propensity to double-park |
| `ridehail_gamma_search` | circling vs. double-parking |

**Two independent hold-outs**, because fitting and reporting on the same data
measures memorisation:

1. **Temporal.** The observation window is split in half. Parameters are fitted
   to the first period only and scored on the second, which sits at a different
   point on the demand profile.
2. **Behavioural.** The illegal-parking rate — a different data source entirely,
   the analogue of a citation record — is never in the objective and is only ever
   scored.

Because the synthetic observations are generated at a *known hidden* parameter
vector, parameter recovery can also be reported directly: we know the answer, so
we can say how close the estimate got. A real study cannot do this, which is
exactly why it is worth doing on synthetic data first.

The observations carry a measurement model too — additive sensor noise and 85%
spatial coverage — so calibration is fitting noisy, partial data, as it would be
in practice.

## Optimization

```bash
python -m src.optimization.optimizer --methods grid random differential_evolution
```

**Decision variable** — the district-wide share of curb stalls by regulation
type, `x = (x_passenger, x_delivery, x_ridehail)` on the simplex `Σx = 1` with
box bounds. The simplex constraint is enforced *by projection inside the
objective*, so every candidate the search proposes is feasible by construction
and no penalty term is needed.

**Objective** — weighted district social cost per run:

```
J(x) = w₁ · passenger search cost      (time × value of time)
     + w₂ · delivery delay cost
     + w₃ · ridehail wait cost
     + w₄ · illegal parking externality (lane blockage, safety, enforcement)
     + w₅ · external cost of cruising VMT
```

Meter revenue and fines are **transfers**, not resource costs, and are excluded
from `J` and reported separately — otherwise a policy that raises revenue by
making everyone worse off would score well.

Three properties of this problem drive every implementation choice:

- **The objective is stochastic.** Each evaluation is a mean over several
  replications with a standard error attached. Improvements smaller than the
  noise are reported as such rather than celebrated.
- **The objective is expensive.** One evaluation is several full simulations, so
  the currency for comparing search methods is *evaluations*, not wall clock.
  Results are memoised on the rounded allocation, and **common random numbers**
  are used — the same seed set at every candidate point, so differences between
  allocations reflect the allocation and not the draw. This is the cheapest
  variance reduction available and it is why a modest seed budget suffices.
- **Selection bias is real.** A search's own estimate of its winner is biased low.
  Every candidate optimum is therefore **re-evaluated on 20 independent
  confirmation seeds** before being compared with the baselines, and those are the
  numbers reported.

**Baselines to beat**, because an optimizer that beats nothing has proved
nothing: today's posted allocation, an equal three-way split, allocation
proportional to curb-*time* demand (arrivals × dwell — the back-of-envelope rule
a planner reaches for first), and the passenger-maximising corner.

**Search methods**: exhaustive grid (complete within its resolution, infeasible
at any finer one), random Dirichlet sampling (a strong control), and SciPy's
`differential_evolution` (global, gradient-free, handles a rugged surface — and
pays for that in evaluations, which is the interesting tradeoff when each
evaluation is a simulation). Polishing is disabled: the local polish step assumes
a smooth deterministic function, which a Monte-Carlo objective is not.

**Multi-objective.** There is no single best allocation, only a frontier. The
Pareto study samples the simplex and labels the non-dominated allocations across
passenger search, delivery delay, ridehail wait, illegal parking and revenue.

## SUMO / TraCI backend

The analytical link-performance function is fast enough for the thousands of
replications calibration and optimization need, but it abstracts away lane-level
dynamics. The optional SUMO backend swaps that layer for microsimulation while
keeping every *decision* in Python:

```
Python                          SUMO (via TraCI)
------                          ----------------
demand generation               car-following, lane changing
curb choice and scoring         link travel times, congestion
search and fallback logic       parkingArea occupancy
ridehail dispatch policy        routing, rerouting, taxi manoeuvres
experiment orchestration        vehicle movement
```

- Curb segments become SUMO `parkingArea` elements — one per regulation type per
  block face, sized from the same inventory the SimPy engine uses.
- Searching is expressed natively: the vehicle is re-routed toward the candidate
  block and given a parking stop there; `parkingAreaReroute` rerouters handle the
  race where a stall fills between the decision and the arrival.
- Double-parking is a lane stop, so it physically blocks the vehicles behind it.
- **Ridehail uses SUMO's taxi device and reservation API.** Persons with a `taxi`
  stage create reservations; Python matches each to the nearest idle fleet vehicle
  and calls `dispatchTaxi`. Pickups and drop-offs are detected by polling fleet
  occupancy. This is TNC simulation, not generic vehicles with a label.

```bash
pip install eclipse-sumo traci sumolib     # bundled binaries, no system install
python -m src.sumo.backend --horizon 90
```

SUMO is entirely optional: `sumo_available()` guards it, its tests skip
themselves when it is absent, and nothing else in the project depends on it. It
is roughly two orders of magnitude slower than the SimPy engine, so calibration
and optimization stay on SimPy and the SUMO backend is used as a cross-check —
see [SUMO backend cross-check](#results).

### What the results say

**The baseline sits at 84.5% metered occupancy**, which is deliberate: that is the
band around which occupancy-responsive pricing is designed to operate, and the
point at which cruising starts to behave non-linearly. The demand sweep shows why
it matters — going from 84.5% to 95.3% occupancy costs 43% more search time and
nearly doubles illegal parking.

**Every reallocation away from metered parking trades passenger search up and
illegal parking down, and social cost falls anyway.** Loading-zone expansion adds
48% to passenger search time but cuts illegal parking by 38% and net social cost
by 13%. That is not a sleight of hand about weights: an illegal stop blocks a
travel lane, and the delay it imposes on everyone else outweighs a minute of
someone's parking hunt. It is the single most policy-relevant result here, and it
is invisible to any model that treats failing to park as simply "not parking".

**The demand shock isolates the cross-modal channel.** Raising ridehail demand
50% barely touches passenger search time (+1.8%, not distinguishable from noise)
but raises illegal parking 52% and social cost 45%. The elasticity table says the
same thing more precisely: ridehail demand has an elasticity of +0.86 on illegal
parking and essentially zero (−0.04) on passenger search. **TNC growth does not
lengthen the parking hunt; it displaces stopping into the travel lane.** A study
that measured only cruising time would have concluded ridehail growth was
harmless.

Passenger demand, by contrast, is the dominant driver of everything (search
elasticity +1.74, illegal parking +1.45, social cost +1.58) — unsurprising given
that passenger vehicles hold a stall for 55 minutes against a TNC's 74 seconds.

**The optimizer disagrees with all three hand-designed policies, and the
disagreement is the interesting part.** Scenarios A, B and D each improve social
cost by 13–16%, and each pays for it by cutting metered parking from 82% to
56–58% — which is why passenger search rises 37–48% in all three. What they also
all do, incidentally, is raise the dedicated TNC share from its scarce 5%. The
optimizer finds that *this* was the binding constraint: hold metered parking
roughly where it is (82% → 80.8%), take the TNC space out of the loading-zone
budget instead (13% → 5%), and get **twice the improvement (−30%) for a 5%
passenger-search penalty instead of a 48% one**, with illegal parking down 61%
and ridehail wait down 17%.

Two honest caveats on that result. First, the delivery share lands exactly on its
lower bound of 5%: the objective as weighted wants even less freight space than
the bounds allow, which is a statement about the weights as much as about curb
policy. Second, freight barely suffers (−2.0% delay) only because commercial
vehicles are modelled as able to use metered stalls, which stay plentiful. Both
are assumptions a reviewer should push on, and both are one config edit away from
being tested.

**Differential evolution earns its cost, but not cheaply.** It found the best
allocation, but used 198 objective evaluations against 65 for the grid and 57 for
random search — roughly three times the simulation budget for an 5% better
objective. On a problem where each evaluation is five full replications, that
tradeoff is the decision, not a footnote.

**Calibration is a worked example of overfitting a simulation model.** The fitted
parameters achieve an in-sample occupancy RMSE of 0.0329 — *below* both the
0.0373 the true generating parameters achieve and the 0.035 standard deviation of
the measurement noise. An estimator that beats the oracle in-sample is fitting
noise, by definition. And it shows: on the held-out window the fitted vector
scores 0.0606 against the oracle's 0.0359, barely better than the uncalibrated
0.0650. Two parameters are badly recovered — compliance (0.51 estimated against
0.78 true) and the ridehail search weight (2.20 against 0.85) — because
segment-level occupancy simply does not identify them: a driver who parks
illegally and one who drives two blocks further both leave the same hole in the
occupancy record.

The fix is not a better optimizer. It is a smaller parameter vector, a joint
objective that includes the behavioural series (the illegal-parking rate is
currently held out; identifying compliance requires fitting to it), and more
seeds per evaluation so the objective is less noisy than the signal being fitted.
This is reported rather than quietly fixed because the diagnosis is the point.

**Parallel scaling is 1.86× on 2 cores (93% efficiency)** — replications are
independent, so the only serial cost is process startup and result collection.
The reference run used a 2-core machine; the numbers above took about 100 minutes
of wall clock end to end, dominated by the 2,115 replications the optimizer
consumed.

**The SUMO cross-check agrees on the mechanism and not on the magnitudes**, and
the reasons are worth stating rather than smoothing over. Curb capacity is
identical by construction (a test asserts it), the taxi device dispatches and
completes real pickups, and the qualitative behaviour is the same. But the SUMO
run reports 22.8% illegal parking against SimPy's 7.7%, and a search time that is
not comparable at all: SimPy starts the search clock when the vehicle reaches its
destination block, whereas in SUMO a vehicle can pass an acceptable parking area
on its way in, so the two clocks measure different events. The illegal-parking
gap is a modelling difference too — the SUMO fallback ladder is coarser than the
SimPy agents', and a vehicle that cannot be given a valid stop for any TraCI
reason burns a search attempt. Aligning them properly means expressing the
fallback ladder once and driving both backends from it, which is the natural next
piece of work. **The SUMO backend is presented here as an architectural
demonstration and a qualitative cross-check, not as a validated equivalent of the
SimPy engine.**

## Testing and CI

```bash
make test        # fast suite
make test-all    # everything, including slow and SUMO tests, with coverage
make lint
```

The tests that matter are the ones that would catch a silent physics break:

- capacity is conserved across a run, and no segment ever goes over-occupied or
  negative;
- a fixed seed reproduces bit-identical output, and parallel execution matches
  sequential exactly;
- warm-up trips are excluded and occupancy sampling stops at the horizon;
- every candidate allocation the optimizer proposes is on the simplex and inside
  its bounds, for arbitrary inputs including degenerate ones;
- the SUMO curb inventory matches the SimPy one stall for stall;
- freight really is more distance-averse than passengers, and ridehail dwell
  really is an order of magnitude shorter — the behavioural distinctions the whole
  model rests on, asserted rather than assumed.

CI (GitHub Actions) runs lint and format checks, the fast suite on Python
3.10/3.11/3.12, the slow and SUMO suites, a real end-to-end integration
experiment (all six scenarios, several seeds, in parallel, plus the report build,
with the outputs uploaded as artifacts), and a Docker build that runs the test
suite inside the image.

## Data and limitations

**The data are synthetic, and deliberately so.** The district is a 5×5 grid of
130 m blocks; demand is generated from configured arrival rates; the "observed"
occupancy used for calibration comes from this same model run at a hidden
parameter vector with sensor noise and partial coverage added. This is a
laboratory for the *method*, not a description of any city.

The simulation engine, the calibration routine and the optimizer never read the
files in `data/` — they exist to pin down the schemas an empirical deployment
would populate: a curb inventory keyed by segment with capacity and regulation by
type, an occupancy series keyed by segment and time window, and a demand table
keyed by class and hour. Loading real meter occupancy, a real curb inventory and
real TNC trip records is therefore a **data-layer change** — write a loader that
produces those same tables — rather than a change to the engine.

Known limitations, stated plainly:

- **No mode choice or destination choice feedback.** Demand is exogenous; drivers
  do not respond to sustained bad conditions by travelling elsewhere, at another
  time, or by another mode. Long-run price elasticities are therefore out of scope
  and the pricing results should be read as short-run only.
- **Enforcement is a per-event probability**, not a patrol process with routes
  and workload. Fines are transfers; the modelled social cost of an illegal stop
  is a parameter, not an estimate.
- **The road network is a synthetic uniform grid** with one lane per direction
  and no signals, transit, cyclists or pedestrians in the roadway. The SUMO
  backend adds lane-level dynamics but not those modes.
- **Parking search is myopic.** Drivers use expected occupancy of nearby blocks;
  they have no memory across trips and no information from an app.
- **Ridehail dispatch is nearest-idle-vehicle.** No batching, no pooling, no
  repositioning strategy. These are the obvious extension points.
- **The optimizer allocates a single district-wide share.** Real curb policy is
  set block by block; a segment-level decision variable is a much larger and more
  interesting problem, and this is the tractable version of it.
- **Calibration is against data the model itself generated.** That makes parameter
  recovery checkable, and it also means the fit is flattering relative to what
  real data would produce: there is no structural misspecification to expose.
